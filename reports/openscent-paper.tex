  \begin{GenesisStructuredAbstract}
    \AbstractBackground{
      Machine learning on olfaction needs molecular structures paired with odour
      descriptors. The descriptor-labelled sets in common use derive from commercial
      flavour-and-fragrance databases under non-commercial terms, which foreclose
      commercial application, tokenised derivatives and independent redistribution.
      The obstacle is licensing rather than science.
    }
    \AbstractMethods{
      Odour assertions were extracted from public-domain sources: 5{,}346 United States
      patent documents and 1{,}790 records for 837 compounds from United States
      government databases (HSDB, CAMEO Chemicals, OSHA, NIOSH) read through PubChem.
      Every molecule name and descriptor was a literal substring of its source
      sentence, re-asserted on every run, and a human decided every accepted row.
      Molecules were identified by structure, not by name text. A term was admitted to
      the vocabulary only if attested in at least twenty distinct documents.
    }
    \AbstractResults{
      The corpus contained 1{,}708 distinct molecules across a 67-term vocabulary; 24
      terms reached the threshold of 30 molecules, and all 24 still do when the 50
      molecules without a resolved structure are removed. Counting name spellings
      instead would have given 27 terms and 1{,}819 names. A 648-compound subset
      supported prediction of odour labels (cross-validated AUC 0.93 for \emph{fatty},
      $n=16$; 0.87 for \emph{mint}, $n=24$). Negative results constrained the method:
      note tiers were absent from patent text; further vocabulary was not the binding
      constraint; the largest reservoir of public odour attributions was
      licence-encumbered; sentence scope limited recall; names were not molecules; and a
      heuristic built to order the review queue was read as a count of what remained in it.
    }
    \AbstractConclusion{
      A permissively licensed structure--odour corpus can be assembled by construction
      from public-domain sources. It is smaller than the encumbered alternatives and is
      reported as such, and its labels carry structure-dependent signal.
    }
  \end{GenesisStructuredAbstract}

\section{Introduction}

Quantitative structure--odour work requires training data mapping molecular structures
to odour descriptors. The dataset most widely used comprises 3{,}523 molecules with
expert-assigned descriptors, curated from the commercial \emph{Leffingwell PMP 2001}
database and released with the reference deep-learning treatment of the problem
\cite{sanchez2019machine}. It is distributed under CC~BY-NC, and its files are
access-restricted, released only on request for non-commercial use \cite{leffingwell2020}.
The consequence is legal, not scientific: a non-commercial clause forecloses commercial
application and tradeable derivatives, and prevents a third party from redistributing a
corrected or extended version. The permissively licensed alternative of Keller and
Vosshall \cite{keller2016}, 480 molecules under CC0, holds panel ratings on fixed
semantic scales rather than curated descriptors, and does not substitute.

We report an attempt to build the missing artefact by construction, from sources
carrying no restriction, so that the licence question is settled before any row exists
rather than negotiated afterwards. Two source classes qualify. United States patent full
text is a government work, and is where modern aroma chemicals, including captive
molecules documented nowhere else, are described. Records of United States government
databases read through PubChem supply a compound identifier, which makes the link from
an odour claim to a structure exact rather than inferred from a name. We ask whether a
descriptor-labelled corpus of usable size can be assembled from these alone, whether
every claim can stay traceable to a verbatim source span, and whether the resulting
labels carry structure-dependent signal.

\section{Methods}

Each procedural rule below is stated with the failure it prevents, because in every
case the failure occurred first.

\subsection{Sources}

Patent text was harvested from 5{,}346 United States documents identified through
classification searches of C11B~9/00 and A61Q~13/00. Non-US patents were excluded
throughout, as they do not carry the absence of copyright on which the licence claim
depends; the European Patent Office interface was used only to \emph{identify} US
documents. Database records comprised 1{,}018 usable Hazardous Substances Data Bank
(HSDB) records over 651 compounds, after six were retired on provenance or attribution
grounds, and 772 odour-clause records from CAMEO Chemicals, OSHA and NIOSH (401, 191 and
180), which add 186 compounds for 837 in all. Sources derived from GoodScents or
Leffingwell were excluded from every stage, as were 30 approved patent rows that only
relayed copyrighted reference works: a patent is public domain, a text it quotes is not.

\subsection{Extraction and adjudication}

\textbf{Verbatim extraction.} Every molecule name and descriptor in a row had to be a
literal substring of its source sentence. A status utility re-asserted this on each run
and refused to report figures when it failed, because a paraphrased row is
indistinguishable from an extracted one once written. Text normalisation was versioned and
applied identically to source and span.

\textbf{Yield before retrieval.} A 200-document sample was retrieved and its yield
measured before any classification was harvested, at about twenty minutes' cost. It
changed the decision three times: C11B~9/00 (1.02 candidates per document) was
retrieved; A23L~27/00 (0.17) and C11D~3/50 (0.36) were declined. Yield alone proved
insufficient: C11D~3/50 yielded twice the declined food class, but about 70\,\% of its
descriptors fell on terms already past threshold and the two terms the sample was meant
to serve, \emph{sandalwood} and \emph{animalic}, did not occur. Target coverage is now
measured beside yield.

\textbf{Adjudicated review.} A human decided every accepted row against written rules;
automated proposals were advisory and automatic rejection was disabled. Blind proposal
accuracy, measured by withholding proposals on 30 rows, was 67\,\%, with errors skewed
towards rejecting valid rows. The rules were written after a blind evaluation disagreed
on 14 of 98 rows, which proved to be two unwritten conventions applied in opposite
directions. The three needing most deliberation: mixtures yield no row, even for
stereoisomers of one compound, since enantiomers may smell differently; compounds named
as comparisons are not descriptors; and negation inverts the claim.

\textbf{Scope.} A row is a single sentence. A narrow passage scope was adopted late: a
descriptor sentence may be paired with the one before it only when that sentence names
exactly one compound and the anaphor can only mean it. Such rows record both sentences,
carry a human-resolved flag, and are counted on a separate line, so that the
sentence-only corpus can be recovered unchanged.

\textbf{Screening.} After batch adjudication, a language model read the remaining
undecided queue and proposed approvals only; nothing was rejected automatically. A human
decided each proposal (220 were accepted), and 50 screened-out rows drawn at random
were put to the human as an audit.

\textbf{Independent attestation.} A vocabulary term was admitted only on appearing in
at least 20 distinct documents with at least 30 attestations, an attestation being a
distinct sentence. Four successive counters were needed, each correcting its
predecessor's blind spot: raw occurrences, documents, a per-term boilerplate ratio, and
passage-level counting. The vocabulary has 67 terms and 130 surface forms; the
surface-form table is human-written and is the only point at which extracted text
becomes a category.

\subsection{Molecule identity}

A molecule is a distinct structure, not a spelling. Patent-side names were resolved to
structures offline with OPSIN and by PubChem name lookup. Of 995 distinct names, 683
parsed as extracted, 69 resolved through PubChem, 15 were read without stereochemistry,
16 were OCR repairs confirmed by a second spelling or by the patent text, and 162 were hand-authorised
rewrites, each stored with its reason (OCR slips, obsolete ring nomenclature,
``X of Y'' phrases); 44 have no definite structure and 6 are held for checking. Two
names merge only when the structure is trusted, so an unconfirmed structure never makes
two molecules one. The identity is the full InChIKey with stereochemistry retained;
database rows are identified through their compound identifier. The corpus span itself
is never altered by a rewrite.

\subsection{Statistical analysis}

Twenty-two interpretable descriptors (molecular weight, log~$P$, polar surface area,
hydrogen-bond donors and acceptors, rotatable bonds, ring counts, element counts and
similar) were computed offline with RDKit from structures resolved through PubChem
identifiers; computing rather than retrieving them keeps the feature table independent of
a remote service's descriptor version. One binary task was defined per label, and
gradient-boosted decision trees were evaluated under five-fold stratified
cross-validation by area under the ROC curve. Labels with fewer than ten positives were
excluded.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Corpus}

Extraction over 5{,}346 documents gave 4{,}676 review rows from 4{,}634 distinct
sentences: a sentence recurring across documents is one assertion, and 42 rows are
per-compound copies of a sentence describing several compounds. Of the rows, 2{,}306
were decided by hand (1{,}136 accepted, 1{,}168 rejected, 2 deferred). The other 2{,}370
were left undecided, not rejected: the model screen read all of them and screened out
2{,}287, set aside 53 whose descriptor word has no entry in the vocabulary table, and 30
are repeats from the same document; no human has read them. With the database records, 8 accepted passage rows and 5 accepted rows found
by targeted search, the corpus holds 1{,}708 distinct molecules, 1{,}658 of them with a
resolved structure.

Twenty-four of the 67 terms reach the threshold of 30 molecules: \emph{acid, aldehydic,
amber, animalic, aromatic, camphoraceous, citrus, earthy, fatty, floral, fresh, fruity,
green, herbal, lily, mint, musk, musty, powdery, pungent, rose, spicy, sweet} and
\emph{woody}. All 24 remain at the bar when the 50 molecules without a resolved structure
are removed; the thinnest, \emph{aldehydic}, has 32 on resolved structures. Nearest below
are \emph{sandalwood} and \emph{tobacco} (28 each; 27 for sandalwood on resolved
structures), \emph{apple} and \emph{anisic} (26 each). The design target was 60--100
terms at 30 molecules each.

Draft~0.2 of this report stated that the corpus had reached 22 terms and that
adjudication had stopped for want of admissible material. That was wrong. At the time
3{,}592 rows were undecided; 1{,}281 of them carried a vocabulary term together with a
trivially named compound that the queue's ranking could not see, and a sample of the 713
with no anaphora, composition or family marker was 47\,\% admissible. Adjudication
resumed in ten batches whose yield fell from 38\,\% in the first four to 10--13\,\% in
the last three; screening the remaining queue added 220 accepted rows and molecules but
crossed no term. The audit found no miss in 50 screened-out rows, which bounds the miss
rate of the screen near 6\,\% (rule of three, 95\,\%): the screened-out rows are not
exhausted, only unproductive.

Counting by name text instead of structure gave 27 terms and 1{,}819 names, against 24
and 1{,}708. \emph{Apple} shows the difference: 34 names but 26 structures, mostly OCR
spellings of one compound. A count is only as good as its definition of one molecule.

\subsection{Structural signal}

Of 651 HSDB compounds, 648 resolved to parseable structures; the three failures were
hypervalent halogen oxidisers rejected by the valence model. Across the 15 labels with at
least ten positives, cross-validated AUC ranged from 0.62 (\emph{sweet}) to 0.94
(\emph{chlorine}). \emph{Chlorine} (0.94, $n=18$) is determined by halogen count and is a
pipeline check, not a finding; labels with 10--12 positives are unstable at this sample
size; and \emph{aromatic} (0.85) carries a semantic confound, the odour term and the
structural property being distinct concepts that correlate. The substantive results are
\emph{fatty} (0.93, $n=16$) and \emph{mint} (0.87, $n=24$). The purpose was negative
control: a corpus of incorrect labels would score near 0.5 and be indistinguishable from a
correct one by inspection.

\section{Discussion}

A descriptor-labelled odour corpus can be assembled entirely from public-domain sources,
and its labels carry structure-dependent signal. It is smaller than encumbered
alternatives; its distinguishing property is that anyone may use it commercially, extend
it and redistribute it. The transferable content is procedural, with six results that
constrain what the sources can be asked to supply.

\textbf{Note tiers are absent from patent text.} Across 60 patents examined there were no
per-compound top, heart or base note assertions: the terms describe accord architecture.
A rule fitted to curated tiers reproduced them at 56\,\% under leave-one-out validation,
marginally above chance for three classes, so tiers are neither sourceable nor inferable.

\textbf{Vocabulary was not the binding constraint.} Twenty-nine further candidates passed
an independence test and were still refused: the best supported reached 8 molecules
against a threshold of 30. Admitting them would have left the numerator unchanged and
worsened the denominator. Separately, the pre-1930 literature was redundant rather than
inconvenient: 27 of 30 classic synthetics already carried a database odour record.

\textbf{The encumbrance is structural, and measurable.} The reference descriptor set is
CC~BY-NC; a second, independent instance appeared when PubChem's \emph{Physical
Description} heading was measured in full: of 124{,}132 annotations, 2{,}923 attribute an
odour to a named compound, covering 1{,}461 compounds absent from this corpus and falling
on terms it is short of (\emph{balsamic}, \emph{nutty}, \emph{apple}, \emph{pineapple},
\emph{honey}, \emph{vanilla}). Seventy-two per cent of that yield comes from the Joint
FAO/WHO Expert Committee on Food Additives under CC~BY-NC-SA~3.0~IGO and was excluded: the
share-alike condition would relicense any corpus incorporating it, and the
non-commercial condition would reintroduce the restriction this work set out to remove.
The part attributable to United States government works, 212 compounds, was ingested.
Where odour descriptions are collected at scale and made freely readable they are often
not freely usable, here by roughly five to one.

\textbf{Sentence scope limits recall, and relaxing it has a price.} 345 sentences carry
descriptors whose compound is named in the preceding sentence. Pairing each descriptor
sentence with the one or two before it gives 3{,}303 and 4{,}849 candidate pairs, of which
861 and 1{,}124 are explicitly anaphoric; seeded samples of 20 and 12 put admissibility
near a quarter, most rejections being composition-level or family references that fail at
any scope. Passage scope changes what a row asserts: a sentence row records that a named
compound is described in a sentence, a passage row that a person decided what ``it''
denotes, and an accurate description bound to the wrong molecule is the characteristic
failure of patent extraction. Only the narrow form was therefore adopted (9 rows, 8
accepted), flagged and separable.

\textbf{A heuristic built to order the review queue was read as a count of what remained
in it.}\label{sec:heuristic} Rows were ranked by a rule requiring a vocabulary term and
a compound-like token, recognised by a digit within a chemical suffix. That is a
reasonable ranking and an unreasonable census, since perfumery materials are often named
trivially (\emph{santalol}, \emph{ambroxide}, Hedione) and carry no digit. When the rule
returned nothing further, adjudication was declared complete. The error surfaced when a
perfumer's palette listed Hedione, absent from the corpus though present in the patents.
It was invisible from inside because the one check made was biased the same way: 25 rows
carrying \emph{sandalwood}, the term then in deficit, gave 1 admissible, but
\emph{sandalwood} attracts composition and marketing prose and those rows were the least
productive corner of the unseen population. A heuristic that orders work correctly will
be read as measuring the work remaining; a count of reviewable rows should be derived from
the adjudication rules, and a check on an exclusion drawn from the whole of what it
excludes. The lesson had to be applied again during drafting: a working note counted only
rows passing a noise filter and recorded \emph{tobacco} as unreachable, when 57 further
rows containing the word had been filtered, not adjudicated.

\textbf{Names are not molecules.} A count by name text overstated both the molecules and
the terms at threshold (27 against 24), through OCR spellings and spacing variants of
one compound. It was found by resolving names to structures, not
by any count of names. Even then, 136 of the 1{,}658 structures rest only on hand
rewrites, OCR repairs or stereochemistry-free readings, which is the first place to look
if a structure is ever questioned.

\begin{GenesisLimitations}
Although encouraging, this study has limitations. Twenty-four of 67 terms reached the
threshold against a design target of 60--100; this is not called a ceiling, since 2{,}370
rows were screened out by the model and left undecided (not rejected) and no human has
read them, but the terms nearest threshold are bounded by the rules as
described and the screen's miss rate is bounded near 6\,\%. One of the 24,
\emph{aldehydic}, depends on a surface-form mapping admitted after its count was known.
Counting only structures read directly from the name or from PubChem, and not those
rewritten or repaired by hand, would give 22 terms. Fifty molecules (44 names with no
definite structure and 6 held for checking) are identified by name text alone. The
structural analysis covers 648 compounds and predates the structures resolved for patent
names and the 186 compounds added from CAMEO, OSHA and NIOSH; it has not been re-run.
Whole-queue precision of the candidate filter is about 0.2; a figure of 0.82 recorded
elsewhere is precision within the subset the ranking favoured. Agreement between
proposals and adjudication is nearly complete in every batch, but the proposal is visible
to the adjudicator, so it measures confirmation rather than accuracy; the blind estimate
is 67\,\%. A text-containment audit found 835 groups covering 1{,}035 documents (19.4\,\%)
to be the same disclosure published more than once, an upper bound; molecule counts are
computed over sets and unaffected, but the document-count criterion for vocabulary
admission has not been recounted. Despite these limitations, the consistency of the
structural signal across independently adjudicated labels supports the corpus being fit
for its stated purpose.
\end{GenesisLimitations}

\section{Conclusion}

A structure--odour corpus of 1{,}708 molecules, 24 of 67 vocabulary terms at the bar, was
assembled entirely from public-domain sources and released without restriction, and its
labels carry structure-dependent signal. For the terms nearest threshold the shortfall is
a result about the sources rather than about effort: the rows that could carry them have
been counted from the adjudication rules, and further terms will mostly need sources not
yet admitted. Earlier drafts concluded that adjudication was exhausted and counted
molecules by name text; each overstated what the data supported, for the same reason: a
convenient measure was read as the quantity itself. The corpus is smaller than licence-encumbered
alternatives, and unlike them anyone may use, extend and redistribute it.

\section*{Data and code availability}

Corpus and ontology are released under CC0~1.0 and code under Apache-2.0, at
\url{https://github.com/VanPez/openscent}. Every row carries its source identifier, the
verbatim sentence, the extracted spans and a licence basis, so that a challenge to any one
source removes the affected rows rather than invalidating the corpus. All headline
figures are computed by a single utility. CC0 waives copyright, not patents: the presence
of a molecule in the corpus says nothing about live patent claims covering it.
